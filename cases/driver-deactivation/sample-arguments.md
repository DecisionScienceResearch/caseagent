## Claim (Daniel Reyes)

I am asking the arbitrator to reverse Celeris Route's decision to permanently deactivate my driver account and allow me to return to the platform, subject to reasonable identity-verification and account-security requirements. I am also asking for $9,000 in lost earnings resulting from the deactivation.

I understand why Celeris was concerned about the facial-verification results. The issue, however, is whether the evidence establishes that I knowingly allowed another person to use my account. I do not believe it does.

The account records show that each facial-verification session followed successful authentication of my account. But Celeris's own authentication records explain that successful authentication establishes that the account session passed the required authentication step. It does not, by itself, establish who entered or possessed the account credentials or who operated the device.

The first incident is consistent with what I explained to Celeris. On June 15, after the first incident, Marco acknowledged that he had used my phone, opened the delivery application, and completed the face check when it appeared. I told him that he could not use my driver account and told him not to do it again.

The second incident occurred on June 21. On June 22, I confronted Marco again. He acknowledged that he had opened the application again, although he said he had only looked at it and had not made any deliveries. I again told him not to log into my account and made clear that it was my driver account and that I did not want Celeris to close it.

The account-security record does not show me providing the new password to Marco or knowingly authorizing later access to the account.

I also did not knowingly provide Marco with the new password after I changed it. Because I had previously used my phone to access my Celeris account, I cannot rule out the possibility that account-access information remained saved or available on the device. I do not know whether that occurred, and the security records do not establish how the July 2 authentication was completed. What I do know is that I changed my password and signed out active sessions on June 22, and I did not knowingly give Marco the new password.

I recognize that the possibility of account-access information remaining available on a device is not established by the security records. I raise it only because the records do not identify how the July 2 credentials were obtained. The records likewise do not establish that I personally entered those credentials or knowingly provided them to Marco.

These facts are important because they show that my response was not simply to deny what happened. When I learned that Marco had accessed my account, I objected to his use. After the second incident, I also took concrete security measures to prevent further access.

The July 2 verification was the third incident relied upon by Celeris. I explained to Celeris that the person appearing in the verification pictures was Marco. The July 2 system record confirms that the account was successfully authenticated and that the subsequent facial verification did not match my identity image. The system record itself does not identify the person who entered or possessed the credentials used for that authentication. It also does not establish how those credentials were obtained or that I knowingly provided them to Marco.

I recognize that the July 2 incident is serious. It occurred after I had already learned about the earlier incidents and taken additional account-security measures. But the fact that another person subsequently accessed the account does not, by itself, establish that I knowingly authorized that access. There is no separate record showing that I gave Marco the new password or knowingly permitted him to use the account after I secured it.

I recognize that three verification mismatches are serious, and I understand why Celeris considered the repeated events when making its decision. But the evidence still does not establish that I knowingly authorized Marco to use my account.

My account history also matters. Before these events, I had no prior account-sharing violations, no prior identity-verification violations, and no prior suspensions or terminations. I had been using the account without this type of problem.

I understand that Celeris has to protect the integrity of its identity-verification system. I am not asking the arbitrator to disregard those concerns. I am asking for a proportionate response based on what the evidence actually establishes.

Celeris's agreement gives it discretion to consider the nature and seriousness of the conduct, whether it was repeated, my prior account history, and the circumstances surrounding the suspected violation. Permanent termination is permitted when Celeris determines that the circumstances warrant it, but it is not automatically required whenever there is a facial-verification mismatch.

Here, the evidence establishes repeated use of my account by someone other than me. It also establishes that I became aware of the problem, objected to Marco's use, and after the second incident changed my password and signed out active sessions. At the same time, I recognize that another facial-verification mismatch occurred on July 2. I am not asking the arbitrator to disregard that fact. I am asking the arbitrator to consider it together with the security measures I took, my clean prior history, and the absence of direct evidence that I knowingly provided or authorized the credentials used for the later access.

For that reason, I believe permanent deactivation was too severe. I am willing to complete another identity verification, maintain a new password, comply with additional security requirements, or take other reasonable steps Celeris considers necessary to protect the account. If necessary, I am also willing to accept enhanced monitoring or a temporary suspension rather than permanent removal from the platform.

I recognize that my historical earnings do not guarantee that I would have earned exactly $4,308.57 every month in the future. For these reasons, I respectfully ask the arbitrator to:

Reverse Celeris Route's permanent deactivation of my driver account;
Allow me to return to the platform, subject to reasonable identity-verification and account-security requirements; and
Award $9,000 in lost earnings, or such lesser amount as the arbitrator determines is reasonably supported by the evidence.

I believe, however, that the evidence supports a less severe response than permanent removal from the platform, particularly given my prior clean account history, my objections to Marco's use of the account, the concrete security measures I took after the second incident, and the lack of direct evidence that I knowingly provided or authorized the credentials used for the later access.

## Answer (Celeris Route, Inc.)

Celeris Route, Inc. asks the arbitrator to uphold its decision to permanently deactivate Daniel Reyes's driver account and to deny his request for $9,000 in lost earnings.

Celeris does not dispute that Daniel's account had a clean prior history. The issue is what happened during the June and July 2026 incidents and whether, considering the repeated account use and the security risk created by those events, Celeris acted within the discretion provided by the Agreement.

Repeated Identity-Verification Failures

On June 14, June 21, and July 2, 2026, the person completing identity verification through Daniel's account did not match the identity image associated with Daniel's account.

Each facial-verification event occurred after successful authentication of the account.

Celeris recognizes that its authentication records do not independently establish who entered or possessed the account credentials. Celeris does not need to establish that Daniel personally typed the credentials on each occasion, however. The relevant question is whether the repeated account activity and identity-verification failures reasonably demonstrated that the account was being used in a manner inconsistent with the Agreement and presented an unacceptable continued-access risk.

Daniel himself identified the person appearing in the verification records as his brother, Marco.

The first incident was followed by Daniel learning that Marco had used his phone and opened the delivery application. On June 15, Marco acknowledged using the application and completing the facial check when it appeared. Daniel told him that he could not use Daniel's driver account and instructed him not to do it again.

The problem nevertheless occurred again.

On June 21, the account was successfully authenticated and another facial-verification mismatch occurred. On June 22, Daniel confronted Marco, and Marco acknowledged that he had opened the application again. Daniel again told him not to use the account and expressed concern that Celeris could close it.

These facts are significant because Daniel had actual notice after the first incident that another person was accessing his account. The problem nevertheless recurred a second time.

Daniel's Security Measures

Celeris recognizes that Daniel took additional security measures after the second incident.

On June 22, after learning of the second incident, Daniel changed the account password and signed out active sessions associated with the account. Celeris does not dispute that those actions are relevant and may be considered in Daniel's favor.

They do not, however, eliminate the underlying security concern.

On July 2, the account was again successfully authenticated, followed by another facial-verification mismatch. Thus, even after Daniel had become aware of the prior incidents and had taken additional security measures, the account was subsequently accessed and used for another identity-verification attempt by someone other than Daniel.

Celeris does not contend that the authentication record independently proves that Daniel personally entered the credentials for the July 2 session. The record does not establish that. Rather, Celeris relies on the overall pattern: repeated authenticated access to Daniel's account, repeated facial-verification failures, Daniel's knowledge that Marco had previously accessed the account, and another facial-verification failure after the account-security measures were taken.

The Agreement expressly permits Celeris to consider account-authentication records together with facial-verification results and other account information when determining whether an account has been used in violation of the Agreement.

The Agreement Places Security Obligations on Daniel

Section 5 provides that Daniel's account is personal to him and may not be lent or otherwise made available for another person's use.

Section 6 requires the Driver to personally complete required identity or facial verification and prohibits knowingly permitting another person to complete the process on the Driver's behalf.

Section 7 also requires the Driver to maintain the confidentiality of account credentials, take reasonable steps to protect the account from unauthorized access, and promptly notify Celeris when the Driver becomes aware of unauthorized access or believes credentials may have been compromised.

Celeris agrees that the evidence does not establish that Daniel knowingly gave Marco the new password after June 22. But the Agreement does not make Celeris's enforcement authority dependent upon proving the precise method by which a later unauthorized person obtained access.

The evidence establishes that the account continued to be used for identity verification by someone other than Daniel. That continued activity was relevant to Celeris's assessment of whether the account could safely remain active.

Permanent Deactivation Was Within Celeris's Contractual Discretion

Section 12 gives Celeris authority to suspend or terminate an account when it reasonably determines that the Driver has violated the Agreement or that continued access presents a risk.

In determining an appropriate action, Celeris may consider the seriousness and repetition of the conduct, the Driver's prior history, information provided during review, and other relevant circumstances.

Celeris considered Daniel's clean prior history. There were no prior account-sharing violations, identity-verification violations, suspensions, or terminations.

That history is relevant mitigation, but it does not erase the repeated incidents at issue here.

This was not a single facial-verification mismatch. There were three separate mismatches over approximately eighteen days, each associated with successful authentication of Daniel's account. Daniel became aware of the problem after the first incident, instructed Marco not to use the account, and nevertheless encountered another incident. After the second incident, Daniel took additional security measures, but another identity-verification failure occurred on July 2.

Celeris therefore reasonably concluded that the account presented an ongoing identity and account-security concern.

Celeris acknowledges that Daniel's objections to Marco's use and his later security measures weigh in his favor. They are circumstances the arbitrator may consider in evaluating proportionality. But Section 12 does not require Celeris to restore an account whenever the Driver disputes knowingly authorizing the access.

The question is whether Celeris's decision was permitted and reasonable under the Agreement in light of the entire pattern. Celeris submits that it was.

Reinstatement Would Not Fully Resolve the Concern

Daniel proposes reinstatement subject to additional verification and security requirements.

Celeris appreciates that proposal, but reinstatement would not change the fact that the account has already experienced repeated identity-verification failures involving another person.

Celeris's concern is not limited to whether Daniel can complete another identity verification today. The concern is whether the account can be reliably associated with the person authorized to operate it and whether unauthorized access can be prevented.

The repeated incidents, including the July 2 incident after Daniel's June 22 security measures, support Celeris's determination that permanent removal was appropriate.

Daniel's $9,000 Lost-Earnings Claim

Daniel also requests $9,000 in lost earnings.

Celeris does not dispute that Daniel earned substantial amounts through the platform before deactivation. His earnings records show:

April 2026: $4,180.25
May 2026: $4,426.80
June 2026: $4,318.65

Celeris also recognizes that Daniel earned $584.40 from July 1 through July 4 before the July 5 deactivation and is not seeking that amount again.

The difficulty is that Daniel's historical earnings do not establish that he would necessarily have earned $9,000 after July 5. His earnings depended on deliveries, demand, availability, incentives, and other circumstances.

His applications and inquiries to other delivery companies show efforts to find replacement work, but they do not establish that Celeris would have generated $9,000 in additional earnings for him or that the entire amount claimed was unavoidable.

Because Celeris maintains that the deactivation was contractually permitted, it also disputes that Daniel has established a recoverable lost-earnings claim resulting from an improper deactivation.

Requested Relief

For these reasons, Celeris Route, Inc. respectfully asks the arbitrator to:

Uphold Celeris Route's permanent deactivation of Daniel Reyes's driver account;
Deny Daniel's request for reinstatement; and
Deny his $9,000 lost-earnings claim, or, alternatively, award only such amount as the arbitrator determines is supported by the evidence and applicable Agreement.

Celeris recognizes that Daniel's prior account history was clean, that he objected to Marco's use after learning of it, and that he took additional security measures after the second incident. Those facts are relevant mitigation. Nevertheless, the account was repeatedly accessed and repeatedly failed facial verification, including after Daniel had become aware of the problem and taken security measures. Under Sections 5–7 and 12 of the Agreement, Celeris submits that the resulting identity and account-security risk reasonably supported permanent deactivation.

## Reply (Daniel Reyes)

Celeris's response correctly identifies that there were three facial-verification mismatches and that the person completing those verifications was my brother, Marco. I do not dispute those facts. The dispute is whether those events establish a contractual violation by me that justified permanent deactivation, particularly after I took concrete steps to secure the account.

1. The evidence does not establish that I knowingly shared my account

The Agreement distinguishes between knowingly permitting another person to use an account or knowingly providing credentials and unauthorized access for which the account holder is not responsible. The evidence does not show that I gave Marco the credentials used for the July 2 access, told him to use my account, or knowingly permitted him to complete the July 2 verification.

The fact that Marco was able to access the account does not by itself establish how he obtained or used the credentials.

2. I did not ignore the earlier incidents

Celeris emphasizes that I knew about the first incident and that another incident occurred afterward. That is true, but the evidence also shows what I did in response.

After the June 14 incident, Marco admitted on June 15 that he had used my phone, opened the delivery application, and completed the face check. I immediately told him that he could not use my driver account and told him not to do it again.

When another incident occurred on June 21, I confronted Marco again on June 22. He acknowledged opening the application again. I again told him not to use my account and specifically told him that I did not want Celeris to close it.

I did not simply continue allowing the problem to occur.

3. I took concrete account-security measures

Most importantly, after the second incident, I took affirmative steps to secure the account.

On June 22, I changed my password and signed out the active sessions associated with the account.

Celeris argues that these measures did not eliminate the security concern because another facial-verification mismatch occurred on
July 2. I recognize that point. But the July 2 event should not be treated as proof that I knowingly permitted the access.

4. The third incident does not establish knowing authorization

The July 2 event is the strongest fact supporting Celeris's position, and I do not minimize it.

But there is an important distinction between:

“Another person accessed my account again”

and

“I knowingly permitted another person to access my account again.”

The evidence establishes the first proposition. It does not directly establish the second.

The July 2 record shows successful authentication followed by a facial-verification mismatch. It does not identify the person who entered or possessed the credentials. It does not establish whether I entered the credentials. It does not establish whether Marco entered them. And it does not establish how the credentials were obtained.

Celeris therefore asks the arbitrator to infer knowing authorization from the repeated access and the surrounding circumstances. That inference is possible, but it is not compelled by the evidence.

5. Section 7's reasonable-security requirement should be considered in context

Celeris relies heavily on Section 7's requirement that I take reasonable steps to protect the account.

I agree that I had that responsibility.

But reasonableness should be evaluated based on what I actually knew and what I did in response.

After the first incident, I told Marco not to use the account.

After the second incident, I again told him not to use it and then changed my password and signed out active sessions.

Those are affirmative security measures. The evidence does not establish that I knowingly gave Marco the new password or intentionally allowed him to circumvent those measures.

Section 7 should not be converted into automatic liability for every unauthorized access that occurs despite reasonable security measures. Otherwise, an account holder could effectively be held responsible for another person's unauthorized conduct even where the account holder objected to the conduct and took reasonable steps to prevent it.

6. Celeris's contractual discretion does not make permanent termination automatic

I recognize that Section 12 gives Celeris significant discretion.

But Section 12 permits Celeris to consider several circumstances, including:

1. the seriousness of the conduct;
2. whether the conduct was repeated;
3. prior account history;
4. information provided during review; and
5. other relevant circumstances.

Those factors do not all point in the same direction here.

2. I had no prior identity-verification violations;
3. I had no prior suspensions or terminations;
4. I objected to Marco's use after learning about it;
5. Marco acknowledged the first two incidents;
6. I changed my password after the second incident;
7. I signed out active sessions after the second incident; and

I am willing to:

4. comply with additional authentication requirements;

8. My financial loss is a consequence of the deactivation

Celeris argues that my historical earnings do not guarantee that I would have earned $9,000 after July 5.

That is why I did not claim that I would have earned exactly the same amount every month indefinitely.

My three complete months immediately preceding the deactivation produced:

April: $4,180.25

## Response (Celeris Route, Inc.)

Celeris's response does not dispute the central facts supporting its decision. The account associated with Daniel Reyes was repeatedly accessed, and on June 14, June 21, and July 2, 2026, the person completing the required facial verification did not match Daniel's enrolled identity image. Each verification followed successful authentication of Daniel's account.

Celeris agrees that the authentication records do not independently establish who entered or possessed the credentials. Celeris does not rely on authentication alone. The decision was based on the combination of repeated authenticated account access, three facial-verification mismatches, Daniel's knowledge of the earlier incidents, and the continued account-security risk presented by the July 2 event.

1. Daniel's Own Evidence Confirms Repeated Unauthorized Use

Daniel emphasizes that he did not knowingly authorize Marco's use.

Celeris does not need to prove that Daniel intentionally handed Marco the password in order to establish the account-security concern that supported deactivation.

The undisputed sequence is:

On June 14, the first facial-verification mismatch occurred. On June 15, Marco acknowledged using the application and completing the face check. Daniel instructed Marco not to use the account.

On June 21, the second facial-verification mismatch occurred. On June 22, Marco acknowledged opening the application again. Daniel again instructed Marco not to use the account.

On June 22, Daniel changed his password and signed out active sessions.

On July 2, the account was successfully authenticated again, followed by a third facial-verification mismatch.

Thus, the account was repeatedly used by someone other than Daniel, including after Daniel had actual notice of the problem and after he took additional security measures.

2. Celeris Does Not Contend That the July 2 Record Proves Who Entered the Credentials

Daniel correctly points out that the July 2 authentication record does not identify who entered or possessed the credentials.

Celeris accepts that limitation.

But that limitation does not resolve the dispute in Daniel's favor.

The evidence establishes that the account was authenticated, the authenticated session proceeded to facial verification, the facial verification did not match Daniel, this was the third such event, and the third event occurred after Daniel had already become aware of prior unauthorized use and had taken security measures.

Celeris is not asking the arbitrator to find that Daniel personally typed the July 2 password.

Instead, Celeris asks the arbitrator to determine whether the overall account activity reasonably justified Celeris's conclusion that continued access presented a security and identity-verification risk under Section 12.

3. The Possibility of a Saved Password Does Not Establish What Happened

Daniel suggests that account-access information may have remained saved or available on his device.

Celeris does not dispute that this is theoretically possible.

But Daniel has not established that a saved password actually existed, that Marco used one, or that this was the mechanism by which the July 2 account was accessed.

C15 expressly does not identify how the July 2 credentials were obtained.

Therefore, the arbitrator should not treat the saved-password explanation as an established fact.

More importantly, the precise mechanism is not necessary to Celeris's contractual determination.

Regardless of how the July 2 credentials were obtained, the account was successfully authenticated and another person again proceeded through facial verification using Daniel's account.

4. Daniel's Security Measures Are Mitigation, Not a Complete Answer

Celeris acknowledges that Daniel changed his password and signed out active sessions on June 22.

Those actions weigh in Daniel's favor.

They also demonstrate why Celeris considered the July 2 event significant.

Daniel had already learned of the first unauthorized use, instructed Marco not to use the account, experienced a second incident, instructed Marco again not to use the account, changed the password, and signed out active sessions.

Nevertheless, another authenticated session occurred on July 2 and again produced a facial-verification mismatch.

Celeris does not argue that this proves Daniel intentionally allowed the July 2 access. It demonstrates something narrower but important: the account remained vulnerable to access by another person despite Daniel's efforts.

Section 12 expressly permits Celeris to consider whether continued access presents a risk.

5. Daniel's Interpretation Would Make the Security Provisions Too Narrow

Daniel argues that because the evidence does not establish knowing authorization, permanent deactivation was unjustified.

But the Agreement contains obligations that extend beyond intentional account sharing.

Section 5 requires the account to remain personal to the Driver and prohibits lending or otherwise making it available for another person's use.

Section 7 requires the Driver to take reasonable steps to protect the account from unauthorized access and to notify Celeris after becoming aware of unauthorized access or believing credentials may have been compromised.

Celeris does not contend that Daniel automatically violated these provisions merely because Marco accessed the account.

Rather, Celeris submits that Daniel's obligations, combined with the repeated incidents and the July 2 access, gave Celeris a reasonable basis to conclude that continued access presented a security risk.

The Agreement expressly allows Celeris to consider these circumstances together.

6. The Three Incidents Distinguish This Case From a Single Mistaken Verification

Daniel correctly notes that Section 6 provides that a single facial-verification mismatch, standing alone, does not conclusively establish that the Driver knowingly permitted another person to use the account.

But this case does not involve a single mismatch.

There were three separate facial-verification mismatches over approximately eighteen days.

The first two were followed by Marco's admissions that he had accessed the application. Daniel then took security measures, yet a third mismatch occurred.

Celeris therefore did not treat one facial-verification error as conclusive proof of intentional misconduct.

It considered the entire pattern permitted by Sections 6, 7, and 12.

7. Daniel's Clean History Was Considered

Celeris agrees that Daniel had no prior account-sharing violations, identity-verification violations, suspensions, or terminations.

That is meaningful mitigation.

But Section 12 does not require Celeris to disregard current repeated conduct merely because the account previously had a clean history.

Celeris considered the clean history together with the seriousness and repetition of the current events.

The question is not whether Daniel had previously complied with the Agreement. He did.

The question is whether, after three separate incidents involving another person's facial verification, Celeris could reasonably conclude that continued access to the account presented an unacceptable security risk.

Celeris submits that it could.

8. Reinstatement Is Not Necessarily an Adequate Remedy

Daniel proposes reinstatement subject to additional safeguards.

Celeris recognizes that this is a reasonable proposal to consider, but it does not eliminate the contractual discretion Celeris exercised.

The account had already experienced repeated identity-verification failures involving another person.

Additional verification could establish Daniel's identity at the time of reinstatement, but it would not necessarily explain how another person repeatedly obtained access to the account or prevent similar access in the future.

Celeris was therefore entitled to consider the continuing integrity of the account rather than merely whether Daniel could pass a new facial-verification test.

9. The Lost-Earnings Claim Depends on the Underlying Deactivation

Daniel's earnings records establish that he earned substantial income from Celeris before July 5.

Celeris does not dispute those historical amounts.

But historical earnings do not establish that Daniel would have earned exactly $9,000 after July 5. His earnings depended on future delivery volume, demand, availability, incentives, and other circumstances.

Celeris recognizes the points that favor Daniel.

Celeris does not ask the arbitrator to find that Daniel knowingly gave Marco the new password or to speculate about how the July 2 credentials were obtained.

Celeris asks only that the arbitrator apply the Agreement as written and consider the complete pattern of account activity, the repeated identity-verification failures, Daniel's prior knowledge of unauthorized use, his security measures, and the subsequent July 2 incident.

Under Sections 5–7 and 12, those circumstances provided a reasonable basis for Celeris to determine that continued access presented an unacceptable account-security and identity-verification risk.

Celeris therefore respectfully requests that the arbitrator:

1. Uphold the permanent deactivation of Daniel Reyes's account;
