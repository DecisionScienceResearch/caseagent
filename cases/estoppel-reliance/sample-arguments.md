# Commercial Estoppel And Reliance Claim: the sample's own arguments

Reference only. These are the human-written arguments that ship with the DecisionLayer sample. caseagent never reads this file: every filing it produces is written by the agent from the dispute, the contract and the exhibits.

## Claim (Vanguard Data Systems Inch)

January 12, 2026: Claimant explicitly notified Respondent via email that it could not enter into a lease or move forward with the site without absolute certainty that a 10MW power upgrade would be live by April 1, stating: "Our servers ship in March and require immediate deployment."

January 15, 2026: Respondent replied via email with an unambiguous promise to secure the tenant: "We have the green light from the utility. You should be good."

February 1, 2026: Driven directly by Respondent's explicit instruction to proceed, Claimant ceased negotiations with a backup facility (forfeiting a $50,000 non-refundable holding deposit) and paid a non-refundable down payment for $1,200,000 in custom server infrastructure tailored precisely to the physical layout of Respondent’s building.

April 10, 2026: Respondent revealed that the power upgrade failed due to local zoning and cost issues, offering only a standard 2MW feed. Because 2MW cannot power the server cluster, Claimant had to walk away from the deal.

## Answer (Hudson Valley Heritage Properties, LLC)

No Enforceable Obligations: The parties were involved in purely fluid, pre-contractual negotiations. No lease agreement was ever finalized, executed, or signed. In commercial real estate, text and emails do not create binding property obligations.

Unreasonable Reliance: Claimant is a sophisticated commercial entity. It is fundamentally unreasonable to forfeit deposits and spend over a million dollars on custom hardware based on a preliminary email exchange before a formal lease contract is fully executed.

Implied Contingency: A major power grid upgrade inherently relies on third-party public utility approval and municipal zoning. Claimant knew, or should have known, that a landlord cannot perfectly guarantee independent government and utility actions.

Inflated Damages: Claimant still holds physical possession of the servers. Seeking hundreds of thousands of dollars is an overestimation, and Claimant has failed to prove it actively tried to mitigate losses by immediately reselling or repurposing the gear.

## Reply (Vanguard Data Systems Inch)

The Pure Role of Equity: Promissory estoppel exists precisely to remedy injustices where a formal contract does not yet exist but a clear promise was made. Respondent cannot use the lack of a signed lease as a shield when their own missing infrastructure prevented the lease from being completed.

Explicit Inducement, Not General Hope: Respondent did not say "we are trying to get the power." They said: "Consider it done. Go ahead and lock in your hardware." This was a direct, unqualified instruction to spend money immediately, completely removing any perceived risk from Claimant's mind.

Actual, Proven Detriment: The damages are fully documented. Certified hardware liquidator metrics prove that because the hardware was manufactured for this specific building layout, it instantly lost 30% of its value ($360,000) the moment the space became unavailable.

## Response (Hudson Valley Heritage Properties, LLC)

Shifting Commercial Risk: Claimant is attempting to use equity to salvage its own poor business judgment. Standard tech industry practice dictates waiting for a fully executed, signed lease before ordering non-refundable, hyper-customized infrastructure.

Negotiations Are Not Promises: Preliminary statements of optimism during a deal negotiation do not amount to an absolute legal guarantee. Respondent acted in good faith, but the utility company ultimately denied the expansion. Claimant chose to jump the gun and take a massive financial gamble before securing the building legally; they must bear the risk of that gamble.

Litigation History: It is clear that in traditional litigation, courts routinely throw out promissory estoppel claims. This is because, as everyone knows, sophisticated parties enter contracts for valuable transactions. We did not have a contract. We had no obligation to tender the site, and our communications to the claimant clearly indicated that we were relying on statements from the Utility - which we obviously have no control over. Though we may have told the claimant to proceed with the purchase - we did not cut their checks or spend their money. They are a sophisticated, large business with millions of dollars and their own lawyers, and they are basically asking this body to bless their incompetence and force us to pay for their impatience. In fact, on the contrary, we believe that they intentionally did not sign the lease because our lease would have - as they know - expressly limited and waived our liability. At best, they are incompetent and should not be rewarded for their incompetence, and at worst, they have intentionally engineered this situation to avoid entering into a contract that they know, full well, because they are not dummies, have completely obliterated any chance they have at recovery.

Also, we did not instruct them to "lock in the hardware" nor did we say "Consider it done." They are making that up. We said "you should be good." Claimant has not provided any emails to support this -because none exist. Our use of 'should' indicates we were not in fact making an iron clad promise. Their whole argument rests on their misinterpretation of our email, which, again, said "you should be good." This is not a promise!
